> Defensive / conceptual section. No lab flags. Useful for **report remediation recommendations** and — flipped around — for **building smarter attack wordlists**.

## Two components of any password policy

1. **Definition** — the *rules* (length, complexity, lifecycle: creation, storage, management, transmission).
2. **Enforcement** — the *technology* that makes compliance mandatory (e.g. an AD Password Policy GPO).

Both are required. A policy nobody enforces is just a document; enforcement with no clear policy is inconsistent. After the technical control is in place it must be **communicated** and backed by **process** so it's applied everywhere.

## Standards to cite (know the names)

- **NIST SP 800‑63B** — modern, influential. Notably recommends **against** forced periodic expiration and arbitrary composition rules; favours length, screening against breached‑password lists, and MFA.
- **CIS Password Policy Guide**
- **PCI DSS** — required where payment‑card data is handled.

> Compliance ≠ security. Standards set a *baseline*, not a guarantee.

## The expiration debate (important nuance)

The old "rotate every 90 days" advice is now widely discouraged. Forced rotation pushes users into **predictable patterns** — `Inlanefreight01!` → `Inlanefreight02!` — which is *weaker*, not stronger. Modern guidance (NIST): **don't expire passwords on a schedule**; force a change only on suspected/confirmed compromise.

## Sample policy (the module's example)

Min 8 chars · upper + lower · ≥1 number · ≥1 special · not the username · (example used 60‑day expiry, debated above).

`Inlanefreight01!` *passes* this policy but is still weak — it contains the company name. This is exactly the mutation behaviour from the **Password Mutations** section, and attackers count on it.

## Blacklist these (defenders add them; attackers prioritise them)

- Company name + words associated with the company
- Month names, season names
- Variations of **welcome** and **password**
- Common weak strings: `password`, `123456`, `abcde`, etc.

<Tip>
**Offensive flip‑side**

This blacklist *is* the recipe for a targeted wordlist. Knowing a target's policy (min length, complexity, **lockout threshold**) lets you prune candidates and pace **password spraying** to avoid lockouts.
</Tip>

## Enforcement in Active Directory

- **Default Domain Policy GPO** sets the domain‑wide password policy (length, complexity, history, max age, lockout).
- **Fine‑Grained Password Policies (PSOs)** apply stricter/looser rules to specific groups (e.g. admins).
- Read the current policy as an attacker with: `net accounts`, `nxc smb <DC> -u u -p p --pass-pol`, or `(Get-ADDefaultDomainPasswordPolicy)`.

## Creating strong passwords

- **Passphrases** beat random gibberish for memorability + strength: `()The name of my dog is Popy!`.
- Caveat: avoid anything **OSINT‑discoverable** (pet/kid names, teams, birthdays) — attackers profile you.
- Generators (1Password, etc.) produce strong but hard‑to‑remember strings → leads into **password managers** (next section).

## Key takeaways

- Policy = **definition + enforcement**; both required.
- Modern best practice: **length + breach‑screening + MFA** over forced rotation and rigid composition rules.
- Forced expiration tends to *create* weak, incremental passwords.
- A blacklist of org‑specific and common words is both a defensive control and an attacker's wordlist blueprint.
- In AD: Default Domain Policy + Fine‑Grained Password Policies enforce it; enumerate it with `--pass-pol` / `net accounts` to guide spraying.
